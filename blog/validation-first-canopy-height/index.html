Random k-fold flattered my model by 3%. A new region cost 45%.

Oct 22, 2026 · 2 min read
  • validation
  • spatial CV
  • conformal prediction
  • remote sensing

I built a small canopy-height model for fire-prone California: GEDI spaceborne LiDAR as the reference, Sentinel-2 and terrain as predictors, XGBoost as the model. The model was the easy part. I spent most of the effort on how to evaluate it, because a fuels map is only useful if you know how wrong it is and where. (Full project write-up.)

Here are the four things that surprised me.

1. Spatial CV barely mattered. Geography did.

Everyone warns that random k-fold leaks information between neighbouring samples. So I used spatial blocks sized by a variogram, plus a 1 km buffer. The result: random k-fold was optimistic by only 2–3%.

Then I trained on one ecoregion and tested on the other. RMSE rose by 34–45%, with biases of 6–9 m. With only 32–40% of the variance explained, neighbouring shots shared little the model could exploit, so the spatial leak was small. The real risk wasn't leakage. It was deployment somewhere new.

Lesson: spatial CV is necessary but not sufficient. If the model will be used outside its training region, test it outside its training region.

2. One feature was quietly breaking transfer

The two regions barely overlap in elevation: 99.6% of coastal shots are below 1,000 m and 92% of Sierra shots are above it. Inside a region, elevation helps a little. Across regions, it forces the trees to extrapolate, and they carry the source region's height level with them.

Dropping elevation cut transfer RMSE by 20% in the Sierra, at a cost of about 1% in-region. An in-region CV score would never have told me to remove it.

3. The simple models were just as good

Random forest beat XGBoost in every evaluation. Ridge regression beat XGBoost on the coast. Within a region all three were within 4% of each other. The flexible models only earned their keep under transfer, where ridge collapsed. If I'd only tuned XGBoost, I'd have reported a more complex model for no gain.

4. Calibration restores honesty, not accuracy

I wrapped quantile XGBoost in conformalized quantile regression. Within a region, the 90% intervals covered 90.2% of held-out shots, and a Mondrian (per-group) version fixed the imbalance between short and tall vegetation.

In the other ecoregion, the same 90% interval covered 56%. Recalibrating on a fifth of the new region's data brought coverage back to 90%, but only by widening the intervals up to 2.4×. Conformal methods guarantee coverage for data like the calibration set. They can't make a model accurate where it has never seen data. That needs local training data, and deciding where to collect it is a ground-truth design question.

What I'd do on any geospatial ML project

  • Report random, spatial, and out-of-region numbers side by side.
  • Audit features for range mismatch between training and deployment areas.
  • Keep a simple baseline in every table.
  • Check interval coverage per group and per region, not just overall.
  • Build a synthetic test bed with known truth first, so you know the evaluation code is right before you trust what it says about real data.
